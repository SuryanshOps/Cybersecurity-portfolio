## 🌐 What is Cybersecurity?

**Cybersecurity** refers to the practice of protecting internet-connected systems, including hardware, software, networks, and data, from unauthorized access, malicious attacks, and unauthorized modification. In an enterprise network engineering context, it is the discipline of ensuring that digital infrastructure remains resilient against data exfiltration and operational disruption.

Modern cyber warfare revolves around highly organized threat actors, automated botnets, and ransomware cartels. Consequently, cybersecurity is divided into two major functional domains:
*   🔴 **Offensive Security (Red Team):** Proactively searching for, analyzing, and exploiting network vulnerabilities.
*   🔵 **Defensive Security (Blue Team):** Hardening networks, monitoring traffic telemetry, and responding to active incidents.

---

## 💡 Why is Cybersecurity Important?

As organizations increasingly rely on cloud infrastructure and connected devices, the attack surface expands. Implementing robust cybersecurity is critical for:

1.  **Data Protection:** Safeguarding sensitive intellectual property (IP), Personally Identifiable Information (PII), and financial records.
2.  **Business Continuity:** Preventing operational downtime caused by disruptive attacks like ransomware or DDoS.
3.  **Regulatory Compliance:** Meeting legal mandates (such as HIPAA, GDPR, or PCI-DSS) to avoid severe financial penalties.
4.  **Maintaining Trust:** Preserving customer and stakeholder confidence in the organization's ability to protect their data.

---

## ⚠️ Common Types of Cyber Threats

To defend a network, engineers must understand the standard classifications of attacks traversing the wire:

*   **Malware:** Malicious software (viruses, worms, ransomware) designed to disrupt or damage systems.
*   **Phishing:** Social engineering tactics used to trick individuals into revealing credentials or sensitive data.
*   **Man-in-the-Middle (MitM):** Attackers intercepting and potentially altering communications between two parties without their knowledge.
*   **Denial of Service (DoS / DDoS):** Overwhelming a target server or network with a flood of traffic, rendering it unavailable to legitimate users.

## 🏛️ The Core Pillars: CIA Triad

Every access control list (ACL) written, every VPN tunnel encrypted, and every firewall deployed maps directly back to the three axioms of information security. 

| Security Pillar | Technical Definition | Network Defense Implementation | Offensive Threat Mapping |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Confidentiality** | Ensuring data is accessed *only* by authorized entities. | IPSec VPNs, AES-256 Encryption, SSH for device management. | **Disclosure:** Packet sniffing (Wireshark), Man-in-the-Middle. |
| **Integrity** | Ensuring data remains accurate and entirely unaltered in transit. | SHA-256 Hashing, HMAC (Hash-based Message Authentication Code). | **Alteration:** TCP session hijacking, payload tampering. |
| **Availability** | Ensuring networks and devices are accessible exactly when needed. | Redundant routing (HSRP/VRRP), EtherChannel, Anti-DDoS appliances. | **Destruction:** Volumetric DDoS floods, SYN floods. |

## 🔐 Security Classifications: Physical vs. Logical

A common vulnerability in network design is over-allocating resources to digital defenses while neglecting physical attack vectors. **A $10,000 Next-Gen Firewall cannot protect a network if the server room door is left unlocked.**

*   🏢 **Physical Security:** Controls protecting the actual hardware (e.g., Mantrap doors, CCTV monitoring, biometric badge readers, rack locks, UPS battery backups).
*   💻 **Logical Security:** Software and hardware logic designed to restrict digital access (e.g., VLAN segmentation, Port Security, Intrusion Prevention Systems).
*   👥 **Administrative Security:** The human element. Password expiration policies, security awareness training, and incident response playbooks.

## 🆔 The AAA Framework: Controlling the Network

Identity has officially replaced the physical network perimeter. The **AAA Framework** dictates how identities interact with enterprise routers, switches, and servers. In enterprise networks, this is centrally managed using protocols like **RADIUS** or **TACACS+**.

### 1. Authentication (Identity Verification)
Proving an entity is who they claim to be. Security engineering mandates **Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)**, requiring at least two distinct factors:
*   *Something you know* (Passphrase, PIN).
*   *Something you have* (Hardware token, TOTP App).
*   *Something you are* (Biometric fingerprint).

### 2. Authorization (Access Control)
Determining what a verified identity is permitted to do. Governed by the **Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP)**. For example, a junior admin may be authorized to use the `show run` command on a router, but strictly prohibited from accessing global configuration mode.

### 3. Accounting (Audit & Telemetry)
Maintaining an immutable forensic trail. Centralized Syslog servers and SIEM systems record exactly who logged in, when they logged in, and every command they typed into the terminal.

## 🏰 Architectural Mindsets: Defense in Depth & Zero Trust

Modern network design rejects perimeter-only security models in favor of continuous verification.

> **🛡️ 1. Defense in Depth (Layered Security)**  
> A strategy utilizing multiple redundant security barriers.  
> *Example:* An attacker bypasses the **Perimeter Firewall** ➔ They are blocked by **VLAN Segmentation** ➔ They bypass the VLAN but are caught by the **IPS (Intrusion Prevention System)** ➔ They bypass the IPS but their payload is stopped by **Endpoint Antivirus**. 

> **🛑 2. Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA)**  
> Operating under the axiom **"Never Trust, Always Verify."**  
> Zero Trust eliminates implicit trust based on network location. Every single packet and resource request is authenticated dynamically, even if the request originates from a trusted switch port inside corporate headquarters.
