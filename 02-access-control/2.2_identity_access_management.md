## 1. What is Identity & Access Management (IAM)?

**Identity and Access Management (IAM)** is the security discipline and framework of business processes, policies, and technologies that facilitates the management of electronic or digital identities. 

While *Authentication* verifies *who* a user is, **IAM** handles the entire lifecycle of that user and strictly dictates **Authorization**—*what* that user is allowed to do once they are inside the network. In the era of cloud computing and remote work, identity has officially superseded the traditional firewall as the primary enterprise security perimeter.

<br>

## 2. The Identity Lifecycle (JML Process)

IAM is not a static configuration; it is a continuous, dynamic business process. In enterprise environments, user identities are governed by the **JML (Joiner, Mover, Leaver)** lifecycle to prevent privilege abuse.

### 🟢 Joiner (Provisioning)
When a new employee or contractor is onboarded, a digital identity is created. They are assigned baseline permissions required to perform their specific job title on day one.

### 🟡 Mover (Role Transition)
When an employee changes departments or is promoted, their access must be dynamically updated. Permissions from their old role must be immediately revoked, and new permissions granted. 
*   *Security Risk:* Failure to revoke old permissions results in **Privilege Creep**, where a user accumulates massive, unnecessary amounts of access over time.

### 🔴 Leaver (Deprovisioning)
The most critical phase for network defense. The exact second an employee is terminated or resigns, all digital access, VPN tokens, cloud portal access, and physical badges must be instantly revoked. 
*   *Security Risk:* Failure to execute this creates **Orphaned Accounts**, which are prime targets for threat actors and malicious insiders.

<br>

## 3. The Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP)

The absolute golden rule of all IAM and access control architectures is the **Principle of Least Privilege (PoLP)**. 

> ⚖️ **Core Definition**  
> A user, program, or system process should only be granted the bare minimum privileges necessary to perform its intended function, and absolutely nothing more.

*   **Why it matters:** If an attacker compromises a user's account, they inherit that user's permissions. By enforcing PoLP, security engineers drastically reduce the **Blast Radius** of a breach.
*   **Enterprise Example:** A Junior Helpdesk Analyst needs the ability to reset user passwords in Active Directory. Under PoLP, they are granted permission to reset passwords, but they are explicitly denied permission to create new admin accounts, delete databases, or modify global group policies.

<br>

## 4. Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)

To enforce the Principle of Least Privilege at scale across thousands of employees, enterprise networks utilize **Role-Based Access Control (RBAC)** as their primary authorization model.

### How RBAC Works
Instead of assigning permissions directly to "John" or "Sarah" (which is an administrative nightmare), network engineers create **Roles** based on business functions (e.g., `HR_Managers`, `DBA_Admins`, `Sales_Reps`). 
1. The permissions are permanently attached to the *Role*.
2. The user is simply placed into the *Role*.

### The Advantage
*   **Massive Scalability:** When 50 new sales reps are hired, IT does not manually configure 50 accounts. They simply add the 50 users to the `Sales_Reps` role, and all necessary access is instantly inherited. 
*   **Simplified Movers:** When John moves from Sales to HR, the admin simply removes him from the Sales role and adds him to the HR role. His access is instantly aligned with his new job.

<br>

## 5. Alternative Authorization Models

While RBAC is the standard, security architects must understand the complete spectrum of access control methodologies:

| Model | Technical Definition | Enterprise Use Case |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 👤 **DAC (Discretionary)** | The creator or "owner" of a file has total discretion to grant or deny access to other users. | Standard operating systems (Windows, macOS) where users share folders. *Low Security.* |
| 🏛️ **MAC (Mandatory)** | Access is strictly dictated by the system. Files are tagged with rigid classification labels (e.g., `Top Secret`), and users are assigned clearances. | Military, Intelligence Agencies, and highly regulated research (e.g., SELinux). *Maximum Security.* |
| 🎯 **ABAC (Attribute-Based)** | Grants access dynamically based on real-time environmental variables (attributes). | **Zero Trust Architectures.** e.g., *"Allow access IF user is in HR, AND using a corporate laptop, AND IP is domestic."* |

<br>

## 6. Directory Services & Privilege Management

To manage these roles and permissions, IAM relies on highly secure, hierarchical databases known as **Directory Services** (e.g., Microsoft Active Directory, LDAP, Entra ID, Okta). Furthermore, strict administrative concepts are applied to secure high-level access:

> 🛡️ **Separation of Duties (SoD)**  
> A fundamental security principle stating that no single individual should have the authority to execute a high-risk transaction from start to finish. *(e.g., The developer who writes the code cannot be the same administrator who pushes that code into the live production environment).*

> 👑 **Privileged Access Management (PAM)**  
> Standard IAM handles regular employees. **PAM** is a dedicated sub-domain specifically designed to secure, monitor, and manage "Superuser" or Root accounts (the IT administrators). PAM systems often utilize secure password vaults where an admin must "check out" a temporary password to access a core router.
