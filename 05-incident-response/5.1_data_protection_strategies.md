## 1. The Value of Data & The CIA Triad

In the modern enterprise, **data is the primary target**. Whether it is customer PII, intellectual property, or financial records, threat actors know that data is the ultimate asset. 

While network defenses (firewalls) and endpoint security (EDR) attempt to keep attackers out, a robust **Data Protection Strategy** ensures that even if a breach or catastrophic hardware failure occurs, the business survives. This directly enforces the **Confidentiality, Integrity, and Availability (CIA Triad)**, with a heavy emphasis on availability and recovery.

<br>

## 2. The Golden Rule of Backups: The 3-2-1 Rule

Having data stored on a server is not a backup. Hardware fails, firestorms happen, and ransomware aggressively searches for mapped network shares to encrypt. Enterprise architecture mandates compliance with the industry-standard **3-2-1 Backup Rule**.

> 📐 **The 3-2-1 Formula**
> *   **3** — Maintain at least **3 copies** of your critical data (1 primary production copy + 2 distinct backups).
> *   **2** — Store the backups across **2 different media types** (e.g., local high-speed NAS storage + enterprise cloud storage or immutable tape drives).
> *   **1** — Keep at least **1 backup copy completely offsite** (air-gapped or in a separate cloud region) to protect against localized disasters or ransomware propagation.

### 🛡️ Immutable Backups (The Modern Defense)
Modern ransomware families do not just encrypt live data; they actively delete or encrypt online backups first. To counter this, enterprises implement **WORM (Write Once, Read Many)** or **Immutable Backups**. Once a backup is written, the storage system mathematically locks it, ensuring that even a compromised administrator account cannot delete or modify the backup files for a specified retention period.

<br>

## 3. Recovery Metrics: RPO vs. RTO

When designing a disaster recovery plan, business executives and IT engineers must define two strict operational metrics:

*   ⏱️ **RPO (Recovery Point Objective):** *How much data loss can the business tolerate?*  
    * *Example:* If your backups run once every 24 hours, your RPO is 24 hours. If the server crashes at 4:00 PM, you lose an entire day's worth of transactions. High-frequency businesses require an RPO measured in minutes.
*   ⏳ **RTO (Recovery Time Objective):** *How fast must the systems be brought back online after a failure?*  
    * *Example:* If an e-commerce platform goes down, an RTO of 2 hours means the engineering team has 2 hours to restore operations before financial losses become catastrophic.

<br>

## 4. Data Loss Prevention (DLP)

While backups protect against data *loss* (availability), **Data Loss Prevention (DLP)** protects against data *leakage* and exfiltration (confidentiality). 

DLP is a comprehensive suite of tools and policies designed to detect and block unauthorized attempts to steal, copy, or transmit sensitive corporate data outside the network perimeter.

### 🔍 How DLP Operates
DLP systems scan data across three distinct states using content-inspection engines and regex pattern matching:
1.  **Data-in-Transit (Network DLP):** Scans email traffic, web uploads, and VPN data packets. If an employee tries to email a spreadsheet containing unencrypted credit card numbers to a personal Gmail account, the network DLP gateway intercepts and blocks the email.
2.  **Data-at-Rest (Storage DLP):** Scans databases, file servers, and cloud buckets (like AWS S3) to find misplaced PII or unprotected intellectual property, alerting administrators to misconfigurations.
3.  **Data-in-Use (Endpoint DLP):** Monitors the local machine. It can dynamically block a user from copying sensitive financial files onto an unencrypted personal USB thumb drive or pasting secure source code into a public AI chatbot.
