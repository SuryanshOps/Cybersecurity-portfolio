## 1. What is Digital Forensics?

When an enterprise cyber incident is successfully contained and eradicated, the post-incident investigation begins. **Digital Forensics** is the scientific process of uncovering, preserving, recovering, and analyzing digital evidence stored in computer systems, hard drives, and networks in a manner that is legally admissible in a court of law.

Unlike live incident response (which focuses on stopping active attacks), forensics focuses on extracting deep, persistent artifacts (deleted files, memory dumps, web histories, and log files) to reconstruct exactly how an attacker operated.

<br>

## 2. The Golden Rule of Forensics: Preservation First

The foundational rule of digital forensics is simple: **Never alter the original evidence.** 

If an investigator boots up a compromised laptop normally, the operating system instantly modifies hundreds of files, writes temporary logs to the disk, and overwrites volatile RAM. This destroys critical forensic artifacts. 

*   **Live vs. Dead Acquisition:** Investigators must decide whether to capture volatile data (RAM and network connections while the machine is powered on) or non-volatile data (the physical hard drive). 
*   **Write Blockers:** Physical or software-based hardware devices that sit between a suspect drive and the forensic workstation, enforcing strict read-only access so that data can never accidentally be written or changed.

<br>

## 3. Chain of Custody (Legal Defensibility)

If a threat actor is identified and hauled into a court of law, or if an enterprise pursues massive legal action, the defense will attempt to discredit the evidence. **Chain of Custody** is the documented, chronological paper trail tracking the seizure, custody, control, transfer, analysis, and disposition of digital evidence.

### 📝 What Must Be Documented:
*   **Who** collected the evidence and **when** (exact date and timestamp).
*   **Where** it was physically or digitally seized (e.g., Server Rack 4, Room B, DC-2).
*   **Every single person** who handled, transferred, or accessed the evidence storage locker.

> ⚖️ **Legal Reality:** If an investigator leaves a hard drive on their desk unlocked overnight, or if the Chain of Custody documentation has a missing 4-hour gap, a judge will declare the digital evidence inadmissible, effectively destroying the prosecution's case.

<br>

## 4. Preserving Disk Image Hashes (Cryptographic Integrity)

How do you mathematically prove that a forensic copy of a hard drive is identical to the original suspect drive, and that nobody tampered with or modified the data during transit? You use **Cryptographic Hashing**.

### ⚙️ The Bit-Stream Image Process
1.  Investigators create a bit-stream image (an exact, sector-by-sector clone of the hard drive, down to unallocated space and deleted files) using forensic tools like FTK Imager or `dd`.
2.  Before touching or analyzing the image, the system runs a cryptographic hashing algorithm (**SHA-256** or **MD5**) against the original suspect drive.
3.  It generates a unique alphanumeric hash digest (e.g., `a1b2c3d4e5f6...`).
4.  After the image is copied and transferred to the forensic lab, the hash is run again on the cloned image.

> 🧮 **Mathematical Proof:**  
> If **Hash A** (Original Drive) matches **Hash B** (Forensic Clone) down to the exact character, it provides absolute mathematical proof that the evidence is 100% untampered and intact. If even a single bit changed during the copy, the hash digest changes completely, invalidating the evidence.
